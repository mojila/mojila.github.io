The cash-plus book

A portfolio that is mostly cash is not an unfinished portfolio. It is a stance — and once the cash pays a real rate, that stance has a price that every risk asset must beat.

Capture 27 November 2026, pre-market Source Pluang MCP Basis USD, cash

01Verdict

The cash is the best-performing asset in the book. At 3.38% a year it earns more than the entire unrealised loss on the equity sleeve, and it does so without a drawdown. The portfolio is really a cash-plus structure: a large yielding cash base and a small four-ETF risk sleeve that is underwater.

That is a conservative profile with a growth kicker — not a growth portfolio that happens to hold idle cash. The distinction matters, because the two are judged against different benchmarks. Measured against a growth benchmark, this book looks badly under-invested. Measured against its own stated stance — preserve capital, earn something on the dry powder, keep a small exposure to equities and metals — it is doing what it says.

The hurdle rate is 3.38%. Any dollar moved out of cash into an ETF must clear that rate after fees and tax to have been worth moving. Before the rate was confirmed, that number did not exist; now it does, and the risk sleeve has to justify itself against it.

03What is not working

  1. The equity sleeve has not beaten cash. It is down against a risk-free alternative that is up. Nothing in the risk book has earned its cost of capital yet.
  2. The metals sleeve is oversized. A third of the silver position is gone; gold and silver moving together means the diversification is thinner than the holding count suggests.
  3. Averaging down into the worst position. Recent flow went into the position already down the most — a mechanical reflex rather than a re-underwriting.
  4. Momentum is negative across the book. Every holding reads bearish on short-horizon indicators. That is not a sell signal on its own, but it is a reason not to add indiscriminately.
  5. Ticket friction is real. Visible purchases run roughly 0.3–0.4% all-in. On small tickets that is about a month of the money's 3.38% yield spent before the capital does any work.
  6. Rate durability is unknown. 3.38% is competitive, but if it is promotional it can reset. At ~1% the cash becomes a drag again and the whole read changes.

04What would change the read

  • If the cash is earmarked for a near-term liability, the allocation is correct and only the equity sleeve should be judged. The cash is not idle; it is committed.
  • If the rate resets toward zero, the hurdle disappears and the case for holding this much cash weakens sharply. The portfolio would need to be re-sized, not just re-labelled.
  • If the goal is growth, the current investable share cannot move total return meaningfully. It would need to rise in steps — deliberately, not by drift.

06Bull, bear, and confidence

Bull case. A yielding cash base with a small ETF kicker is a genuinely defensive, low-drawdown structure. Cash interest has already offset the equity loss more than once on an annualised basis.

Bear case. The equity sleeve is a hobby allocation — too small to matter if right, large enough to lose a third of a position if wrong. Four funds, a quarter in metals, and mechanical averaging down.

Confidence. High on balances, weights and unrealised P&L. Medium on the 3.38% — it is assumed to apply to the USD cash balance, and there is no yield endpoint to verify it. Low on historical return: only unrealised P&L is knowable, with no equity curve and no realised P&L.
